New research on cyberbullying & bystanders

Most of us know cyberbullying is wrong.
Almost none of us know what to type.

Researchers at Cornell built an AI chatbot that helps bystanders write the comment, not just decide to care. Here's what happened when 44 people tried it.

00 — The problem

Everyone agrees you should say something. So why doesn't anyone?

When cyberbullying happens in public — a cruel comment thread, a pile-on, a screenshot passed around — the people who see it and say nothing outnumber the people who speak up. Decades of research call this the bystander effect, and it applies just as strongly online as it does in person. But telling people to "be an upstander" hasn't solved the problem, because caring was never really the missing piece.

Psychologists have long described intervening as a five-step mental process. Each step is its own hurdle:

1

Notice something is happening

2

Decide it's serious enough to count

3

Feel it's your responsibility to act

4

Figure out how to act

5

Actually do it

Almost all existing anti-cyberbullying tools focus on the first three steps — helping people notice, care, and feel responsible. This study asks what happens at step four, the one right before action, which turns out to be where most people quietly give up.

01 — The study

Pairing up strangers to role-play a cyberbullying scene

The research team, led by Haesoo Kim at Cornell, ran two studies back to back. The first was designed to observe what actually goes wrong when someone tries to intervene, in as realistic a setting as they could ethically build.

How the first study worked

24 college-age participants were paired up. One person played the "bystander," reacting to a simulated social media post showing a cyberbullying scenario — things like racist comments, public shaming, or someone being left out of a group photo online. The other person secretly played an AI chatbot "co-pilot," coaching the bystander through the situation via chat.

The bystanders were told they were talking to an actual chatbot. In reality, a trained human on the other end was trying to guide them toward speaking up publicly, the way a good AI assistant might. Every conversation was followed by an interview asking each person to walk back through their own thinking, moment by moment.

This wasn't a survey asking people what they'd theoretically do. It put them in front of a real, upsetting-looking post and asked them to actually respond, live, with a partner watching.

02 — What went wrong

People knew what to do. They just froze on how to say it.

The biggest surprise: almost nobody was confused about their options. Most bystanders already knew they could comment, message the victim, or report the post. What stopped them was sitting down to actually write the words — and once that got hard, it dragged old doubts back up with it.

✍️

Writing the message

Many participants had never written a public, supportive comment before and didn't know how to phrase one without sounding awkward or confrontational.

🎯

Doubting it would work

Even people ready to act worried a comment wouldn't change anything, which made the effort feel pointless before they'd tried.

🙋

Respecting the victim

Some held back because they weren't sure the person being targeted would even want public support drawing more attention to the situation.

⚠️

Fear of backlash

Others worried that speaking up would make them the bully's next target, or that their comment could be twisted and cause more harm.

And here's the twist: hitting one of these "how" walls didn't just stall people out. It made them second-guess decisions they'd already made — like whether it was really their problem, or whether the situation was actually bad enough to warrant getting involved.

Anything can happen online, and it might be that people who see this are very unwilling to stand out. — a study participant, on doubting whether a comment would matter

One participant had already agreed to help, but backed out of a public comment the moment they weren't sure whether to identify who was doing the bullying:

Why would I get unnecessarily involved if I'm not close to her, and publicly? — a study participant, reconsidering their responsibility to act

What actually helped, in the moment

Watching the human "co-pilots" in action, the researchers noticed a handful of conversational moves that reliably nudged bystanders forward:

💬

Asking, not telling

Questions like "what do you think is happening here?" got people thinking for themselves instead of just accepting instructions.

🔍

Pointing out details

Noting specifics — like an imbalance in who liked which comment — helped bystanders see the situation more clearly as unfair.

🔮

Naming the cost of silence

Pointing out that staying quiet also has consequences reframed inaction as a choice, not a neutral default.

🪜

Starting small

Asking someone to commit to a tiny step first, like flagging a post, made a bigger step feel more reachable next.

03 — The fix

ConCUR: a chatbot that starts with "what would you say?"

Armed with these insights, the team built ConCUR (short for Conversational Collaborative Upstanding in Real-time), a prototype chatbot meant to actually be used, not just studied from the sidelines.

Most anti-bullying programs try to build motivation first, then get to the practical "how" much later, if at all. ConCUR flips that order. The very first thing it asks a bystander is some version of: what would you say in response to this? It then works with them to co-write an actual comment, live.

The old approach

Build empathy and a sense of responsibility first.

Hope the person eventually figures out the right words on their own.

Treat "deciding to care" and "knowing how to act" as two separate problems, solved in order.

ConCUR's approach

Ask the person to start drafting a message immediately.

Let doubts about motivation, safety, and effectiveness surface naturally through that writing process.

Address all of those doubts together, as they come up, instead of one at a time.

The logic: writing a real message forces someone to confront their actual hesitations right away, instead of stopping at a comfortable, abstract "yes, I agree bullying is bad." A half-written comment is a much more honest prompt for "wait, should I even post this?" than a hypothetical question ever could be.

04 — Did it work?

20 students tried ConCUR. Most of them actually posted.

18/20
participants ended their session by writing and publicly posting a comment they said they'd genuinely consider posting in real life.

That's a notably high number for the hardest, riskiest form of intervention — a public comment, with your name attached, in front of strangers. Participants described the chatbot's gradual, question-first style as what made the difference, saying it felt more like being guided than being instructed.

What made it more trustworthy was the fact that it guided me, instead of telling me what to say. — a study participant, on the chatbot's coaching style

Interestingly, people didn't need to think the chatbot was human-like for it to work. Several participants said it sounded obviously "chatbot-y" — and used that as a reason to rewrite its suggestions in their own voice, rather than as a reason to give up. The AI's imperfection nudged people to take more ownership of the final message, not less.

Where it didn't work

Two of the twenty participants never posted anything publicly. Both felt that public comments simply don't change anything on today's social platforms, where people can hide behind anonymous or throwaway accounts. They argued that reporting a post, or getting an account suspended, would carry real consequences — a comment, in their view, would not.

The takeaway isn't that they were wrong. It's a reminder that no single intervention style works for everyone, and that as online platforms and bullying tactics keep changing, the "right" way to help will keep changing too.

05 — Why it matters

The real barrier to helping isn't caring. It's not knowing what to type.

Step back from the chatbot itself, and this research points to a simple, useful reframe for anyone who's ever frozen up watching something bad happen online:

✅

Motivation usually isn't the problem

Most people already believe cyberbullying is wrong and already feel they should do something. Guilt-tripping or "raising awareness" targets a barrier that, for many, doesn't really exist anymore.

⌨️

The blank text box is the real wall

Not knowing how to phrase a supportive, non-confrontational comment is often what actually stops people, more than fear or apathy.

🔁

Doubt isn't a one-time hurdle

Deciding to help doesn't settle the question for good. New doubts can resurface right up until the moment of action, and support needs to handle that.

🛠️

A small nudge to draft something can be enough

Getting someone to write even a rough first attempt at a supportive message seems to unlock the rest of the decision on its own.

For parents, teachers, and platform designers, the practical suggestion here is refreshingly concrete: instead of another lecture on why bullying is bad, try handing someone a starting sentence and asking them to finish it. The researchers argue that future tools — whether chatbots, browser extensions, or classroom exercises — should be built around this same idea: help people write the thing, and the willingness tends to follow.

The study was conducted in a lab with college-age participants over short, one-time sessions, so it's not yet known whether these effects hold up in the messiness of real life, or over the long term. The team is continuing this line of work to find out.
