What makes something Professional enough to be on LinkedIn?

Young adulthood is often a time for exploration – you are transitioning from a time as a ‘student’ and working your way towards becoming a professional, looking for work, and finding your place in society. LinkedIn, a social media platform for professional development and job-searching, is one resource that explores this topic.

Still, adjusting to and learning how to use LinkedIn effectively introduces added challenges. We are often used to sharing personal and fun details on social media, but LinkedIn is not like that. Questions such as ‘What are things that feel too personal to post on LinkedIn?’ or ‘Is it unprofessional to post about personal issues?’ arise alongside it.

And so we set out to answer this question: What are the ways we can maintain professional appearances on LinkedIn while making sure that we don’t sound fake and robotic?

We at the Cornell Social Media Lab ran a study with 20 students to see how they perceive personal information on LinkedIn. We set out to explore how users learn what types of behavior is appropriate to appear professional, how they judge others' based on these norms, and finally how this impacts how college students use LinkedIn. What we found is that there is an idea of bounded and controlled legibility that influences what sort of disclosures are considered appropriate.

00 — The Background

What makes LinkedIn so special?

Linkedin is a unique social media environment in that the platform prioritizes professional self-presentation rather than personal communication. LinkedIn explicitly focuses on professional features such as networking, recruiting, and career advancement, encouraging users to post curated and polished content.

🪪 Professional and Polished

LinkedIn explicitly focuses on professional features such as networking, recruiting, and career advancement. This design encourages users posting curated and polished content.

🎤 Larger Imagined Audience

Anyone on LinkedIn can see your profile, by design. Thus, LinkedIn users imagine a larger audience: full of weak ties, but also allowing people to reach out further.

👓 Public Image

The publicness of LinkedIn can have some benefits, such as reducing deception and holding people socially accountable. However, users may also employ various methods to manage and curate their public appearance.

01 — The Question

What determines personal and professonal on LinkedIn?

We at the Cornell Social Media Lab ran a study with college and graduate students to see how they perceive personal information on LinkedIn. Many factors impact what people see as appropriate or not appropriate to be posted on a platform, and LinkedIn is no exception.

In fact, LinkedIn is a particularly useful context to explore self-presentation on social media, due to its uniqueness in context and public nature. Due to its widespread use, it also becomes a representation of how people perceive social norms in professional settings.

What did we study?

We set out to explore how users learn what types of behavior is appropriate to appear professional, how they judge others' based on these norms, and finally how this impacts how college students may use LinkedIn. Going beyond a one-size-fits-all self presentation model, we looked at what the specifics of the platform enabled.

Research Questions
RQ1

What are the perceived norms for professional appropriateness on LinkedIn?

RQ2

Who shapes users’ sense of what counts as “too personal”?

RQ3

Which cues in others’ professional outputs make content seem “too personal,” and how do those cues shape both judgments of others and users’ own self-presentation?

RQ4

How do these boundary judgments translate into composing strategies for users’ profiles, posts, and professional personas?

Study Methods

We spoke with 20 Cornell students, ages 18–25, over Zoom. Each interview took around 45–60 minutes. We asked about their experience with LinkedIn, walking through their real LinkedIn feeds, profiles, and posts while participants narrated their thoughts and decision-making processes.

Interview protocol (45–60 min, over Zoom)
Warm-up.

Why they made an account, what they mainly use it for, and who they imagine is watching when they post.

Defining Baseline.

In the participants' own words: what counts as "appropriate," and what does "personal" mean on this platform?

Consuming Content.

Praticipants were asked to scroll through their LinkedIn feed and think aloud on: What stands out? How they percieve and judge others'? How does this impact their own behavior?

Curating Profiles.

How does a user curate their profile, and what/who influenced this decision?

Posting & commenting.

What type of content do they interact with publicly (posting, reposting, commenting), and what influenced their decision making process?

Wrap-up.

Which group of people most shapes their sense of "too personal," and whether they'd revise their earlier definition now that they've thought it through out loud.

Approved by Cornell's Institutional Review Board. Participants could skip questions, stop screen-sharing, or blur any sensitive detail at any point.
02 — What are the percieved social norms in LinkedIn?

Distinguishing personal and professional content in LinkedIn

Our participants described LinkedIn as a space with mixed and evaluative audiences, with recruiters, employers, mentors, coworkers, and peers all present at the same time. This made linkedin a more purpose-driven platform than a relationally-driven one. These expectations made their LinkedIn use more intentional, especially compared to more social and recreational platforms such as Instagram.

"

Every move you make on LinkedIn [...] is a reflection of your professionalism.

— P13

This distinction shaped their understanding of what is 'appropriate' or 'professional'. The potential professional consequences of LinkedIn use was more demanding than simply not being 'too personal'.

Participants thought that 'personal' content on LinkedIn is appropriate when...

Content is:

Professionally relevant

Content that connects to career direction and professional goals, rather than unrelated personal updates. However, 'Personal' content was better received when it was anchored to a recognizable professional purpose.

“[Relevant content] is experience that helps with […] job prospects” (P4)
“[LinkedIn] should show professional-related stuff rather than unrelated personal updates” (P14)
Content is:

Useful to others

Personal content was seen more appropriate when it was used to share useful information, such as opportunities, advice, lessons, or reflections.

“sharing something that you think others will find valuable to their growth [...] inspiring to their career growth” (P2)
“[Personal stories] could work when talking about authentic experiences while relating that back to the workplace” (P9)
Content is:

Polished in Language and Format

LinkedIn content was generally expected to have 'professional' writing, with correct grammar, concise wording, and conventionally formatted text. This led to the emergence of formulaic posting patterns.

“making sure all of your language is… good grammar, and also pretty polished” (P14)
“professional work tends to value conciseness and gravity” (P7)
Content is:

Humble while highlighting achievements

While LinkedIn was considered a place to share one's achievements, they could be percieved as bragging if they were not presented carefully. Posts distributing credit to others rather than centering the individual helped users appear more credible and relational.

“it’s always better to show your appreciation to the people that helped you along the way.” (P2)
03 — How are these norms created?

Norms are taught, modeled, and internalized.

It is important to note that these norms and standards were not born out of a vacuum — rather, a combination of direct coaching, platform cues, and engagement with broader professional culture was involved.

While users typically learn to interact with platform norms through observing cues and user behaviors in social media platforms, our participants noted that LinkedIn was unique in that there was often a direct coaching element involved. Career-focused classes, student organizations, mentors both professional and personal – all of these impacted how participants understood and interacted with LinkedIn content.

04 — Composing the Professional Self

LinkedIn as a business card: controlled legibility

Understanding these boundaries between being personal and professional centered on restraint. Instead of maximizing self-expression and disclosing various parts of oneself, a strategy of just enough was commonly used. In this way, LinkedIn was a professional entry point rather than a full view of yourself.

"

I see LinkedIn as a way almost like a business card […] where people would look at after they’ve met me.

— P15

A business card does not include the full details about who you are, what your experiences are, and how you perform. By this logic, your LinkedIn profile need not include everything about your life and your accomplishments. Instead, it could be used as a way to verify basic professional information, so that richer conversations could happen elsewhere – such as an interview or a coffee chat.

What do you get from limiting information on LinkedIn?

🪪 Gateway to richer conversations

LinkedIn was used as a way to verify basic professional information, so that richer conversations could happen elsewhere --- such as an interview or coffee chat

🤖 Flexibility reduces risk

Detailed LinkedIn profiles could reveal too much too quickly, lead to audiences making assumptions or stereotyping the user before they can present their own story.

🤖 Avoiding unnecessary attention

Many participants would prefer making a profile update to show an achievement rather than making a separate public post. When they did, they rarely deviated from established patterns.

An important thing to note is that being genuine is not necessarily the same thing as being vulnerable. A post could be authentic without necessarily mentioning the difficult or intimate aspects of the experience. Highlighting positive experiences is different from fabricating a completely new experience, or pretending to be someone that they're not.

The Stereotypical Post Formula

Participants pointed out that there were posts that uses a recognizable, 'stereotypical' LinkedIn structure: announcing the achievement, expressing gratitude, mentioning who helped, and often accompanied by visual marker such as a company photo or logo. By conforming to this formula, we can reduce the risk of seeming too personal or different, focusing on appearing credible and 'conventional'. This can increase your professional visibility while avoiding risks of oversharing.

John Smith . You
Incoming [position] at [job]

I'm very excited to announce that I will be joining [job] as a [position] this year!

> Detail what you have achieved at the beginning of the announcement.

I am grateful to everyone who made me feel so welcome, appreciated, and taught me everything to thrive and get out there in this new journey.

> Express gratitude, frame the achievement as a collective effort rather than an individual one

Special thanks to my internship mentor [name] for the support and encouragement throughout this process. Looking forward to new adventures!

> Directly mention those who helped and supported you.
> An eye-catching visual marker such as a photo or logo.

06 — Going forward

What can we learn from this?

In short, determining what is professional and what is personal is a delicate balancing act, a negotiation between how we can make it useful and relevant versus just being personal. Here we organize a few takeaways we have learned from our study; you can apply it to your LinkedIn use to understand how you might present yourself more professionally.

  1. Personal content should be relevant, useful, polished and humble to be seen as part of a professional identity. Don’t shy away from sharing information just because it is not work-related: if you can tie it into your work story, it can even help you.
  2. Conceptualize your LinkedIn profile as a ‘business card’: People can and will get to know you better once they notice you through your profile. Standardized post and profile formats can help you avoid unnecessary attention while sharing your achievements.
  3. Seek guidance from your peers and mentors on how to present yourself. You are not alone in figuring this out. While you can learn from just watching and observing, there are many professional resources available as well as people who have gone through this process that are willing to help you. Professional clubs can be a good resource, as well as asking your family and friends.
