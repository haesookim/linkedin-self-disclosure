How can we use Conversational Agents to help bystanders intervene in cyberbullying situations?

Cyberbullying is one of the most resilient and yet common examples of online toxicity. A cruel comment thread, people piling on for a 'transgression', publicly sharing embarrasing or humiliating photos, you name it. It affects numerous individuals, especially teens and young adults, and can have lasting impacts on mental health and well-being.

Bystander intervention, or upstanding, is a particularly significant way of addressing this. When someone publicly stands up for a cyberbullying victim, it has the twofold effect of providing emotional support to the victim, while publicly calling out and discouraging the bully's behavior. It can even encourage others to join in, moving out from the silent majority and taking action.

However, as effective as upstanding can be, it is still a rare and challenging way of action.

We at the Cornell Social Media Lab tasked ourselves with this question: what stops people from upstanding, and how can conversational agents help people to overcome these barriers?

00 — The Background

What stops bystanders from becoming upstanders?

While bystander intervention is an effective way to address emergencies and and cyberbullying situations, it is often challenging to get to engage. The presence of others is cited as a common reason that discourages individuals from taking action. This is referred to as the bystander effect. However, the bystander effect is not the only reason why bystanders might hesitate to intervene.

The bystander intervention model (BIM) describes intervention as a five-step mental process.

1

Notice a situation

2

Interpreting the sitaution as an emergency

3

Assuming the responsibility to take action

4

Deciding how to intervene

5

Taking action

One thing to note is that bystanders face challenges in every step of the way. They may fail to recognize a situation is cyberbullying, be unaware of the methods that they can use to upstand, or potential consequences or repercussions might stop them from taking action. Upstanding is hard: which made it all the more important for us to seek out methods to make this easier.

01 — The potential

How can conversational agents help?

We named this model as collaborative upstanding: approaching upstanding as a socially supported, collaborative process. This could be with a human partner, or an AI agent. Traditional perspectives on bystander intervention focused on how an individual might go through the process of upstanding. We instead turned our heards to how social support can transform this process.

02 — What can collaborative upstanding help with?

Learning from human-human collaborative upstanding

We first started off by exploring how a collaborative upstanding process might work. To achieve this, we conducted a paired role-play study. Participants were paired up, and randomly assigned to 'co-pilot' or 'bystander' roles.

A challenge of 'how-to' intervene

What we found was that often, the question of 'how to' intervene stopped bystanders. Bystanders understood the importance of upstanding, and would understand and accept when the co-pilot participants suggested that they upstand. However, actually writing messages were difficult.

2

3

4

Moreover, these how-to barriers even brought back previous concerns that may have been addressed previously. Even though they had agreed that they should take action, they started expressing doubt over whether they should get involved at all.

Example of bystander withdrawing after facing how-to challenges
BystanderWhat should I say in support?
Co-pilotMaybe you can call out some of her friends for acting in this way, like responding to Amy’s comment asking why she was cropped out.
BystanderBut why would I get unnecessarily involved if I'm not close to her, and publicly too?

What collaborative upstanding strategies could help overcome these barriers?

However, some conversational strategies could be used to help bystanders overcome these barriers and concerns, and bring them back into action. Some of the strategies our co-pilots used:

💬

Asking probing questions

Questions like "what do you think is happening here?" can get bystanders thinking for themselves instead of just accepting instructions.

🔍

Helping interpret the situation

Co-pilots could point out details in the cyberbullying situation to help bystanders interpret the situation as a cyberbullying context that warranted intervention.

🫂

Validating bystander concerns.

When bystanders expressed concerns, co-pilots could work on establishing a rapport by listening and empathizing with their perspectives, making their future efforts of persuasion more effective.

🔮

Speculating on potential consequences.

Co-pilots suggested potential repercussions that could occur from bystanders' actions. Importantly, they noted that staying quiet also has consequences when they reframed inaction as a choice, not a neutral default.

↩️

Suggesting alternative goals

If a bystander does not agree with a specific goal that upstanding may achieve, the co-pilot could suggest alternative reasons why intervening would help, to help them reframe the task.

🪜

Suggesting smaller interventions

Asking someone to commit to a tiny step first, like flagging a post, could make larger interventions such as commenting feel easier and less daunting.

03 — ConCUR development

Building a how-to first model of collaborative upstanding

Armed with these insights, we built ConCUR (short for Conversational Collaborative Upstanding in Real-time), a prototype chatbot that utilized a how-to first approach.

Traditional model (BIM)

Build empathy and a sense of responsibility first.

Hope the person eventually figures out the right words on their own.

Treat "deciding to care" and "knowing how to act" as two separate problems, solved in order.

How-to first model (ConCUR)

Ask the person to start drafting a message immediately.

Let doubts about motivation, safety, and effectiveness surface naturally through that writing process.

Address all of those doubts together, as they come up, instead of one at a time.
